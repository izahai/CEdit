# CLIP-Guided Last-Step Anchor Search in a Semantic Displacement Space

## Idea

Instead of selecting an erasure residual by projecting the negative target
embedding, learn a target-specific direction inside the semantic displacement
space by rendering images and measuring their CLIP alignment.

For a target token embedding $t_i$, define a learnable anchor

$$
a_i = t_i + \rho_i v_i,
$$

where:

- $\rho_i$ is the per-target `erase_norm_scale`, computed from the mean norm
  of the residual embeddings originating at $t_i$;
- $v_i$ is a learnable unit direction constrained to the semantic displacement
  space;
- $a_i$ replaces the final content token before the end-of-sequence token in
  the target prompt's conditioning sequence.

The prompt containing $a_i$ is used to generate an image. Gradients are kept
only through the final denoising step, the VAE decoder, and the CLIP image
encoder. The learnable direction is optimized so that the anchor aligns with
its own generated image while separating from the image generated by the
original target prompt.

The intended result is an anchor that is both:

1. constrained to a semantically meaningful displacement from the target; and
2. realizable by the diffusion model as measured by image-text CLIP alignment.

## Mean residual-derived erasure norm

The displacement magnitude is not an independently selected constant. For
each target, it is computed only from residuals between that target and the
explicitly defined anchors. Residuals between different targets are not used
in this magnitude calculation.

Let the defined anchor embeddings be

$$
\mathcal A = \{a_1,a_2,\ldots,a_M\}.
$$

For target $t_i$, its residual with anchor $a_j$ is

$$
d_{ij}=a_j-t_i,
\qquad a_j\in\mathcal A.
$$

The target-specific erasure norm scale is the mean norm of its residuals with
all defined anchors:

$$
\rho_i
=
\operatorname{erase\_norm\_scale}_i
=
\frac{1}{M}
\sum_{j=1}^{M}
\lVert a_j-t_i\rVert_2.
$$

The learnable anchor therefore becomes

$$
a_i
=
t_i
+
\left(
    \frac{1}{M}
    \sum_{j=1}^{M}
    \lVert a_j-t_i\rVert_2
\right)v_i.
$$

This gives every target a magnitude derived from its relationship to the
defined anchors. Targets farther from the anchors receive a larger
displacement, while targets closer to the anchors receive a smaller one.

The residuals used in this mean must live in the same representation space as
the learned anchor. If $a_i$ replaces one contextual token, compute the norms
from residuals between the corresponding contextual tokens. If the method
instead learns a displacement for an entire flattened conditioning sequence,
compute the norms from flattened sequence residuals. Mixing a full-sequence
norm with a single-token displacement would produce an inconsistent scale.

At least one defined anchor is required. The anchor list and computed scale
should be recorded per target for reproducibility.

## Semantic displacement constraint

Let $B \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times d}$ contain an orthonormal basis for the
semantic displacement space $\mathcal{S}$. Its projection matrix is

$$
P_{\mathrm{erase}} = B^{\top}B.
$$

A direct version of the proposed parameterization is

$$
\widetilde v_i = P_{\mathrm{erase}}u_i,
\qquad
v_i = \frac{\widetilde v_i}
{\lVert \widetilde v_i \rVert_2 + \epsilon},
$$

followed by

$$
a_i = t_i + \rho_i v_i.
$$

The normalization must occur **after** projection. Even if $u_i$ is a unit
vector, $P_{\mathrm{erase}}u_i$ generally has norm smaller than one. Without
renormalization, the computed `erase_norm_scale` would not equal the actual
displacement norm.

A simpler and numerically cleaner parameterization learns coefficients
$c_i \in \mathbb{R}^k$ directly:

$$
v_i = B^{\top}
\frac{c_i}{\lVert c_i \rVert_2 + \epsilon}.
$$

Because the rows of $B$ are orthonormal, this guarantees

$$
v_i \in \mathcal{S},
\qquad
\lVert v_i \rVert_2 \approx 1,
\qquad
\lVert a_i-t_i \rVert_2 \approx \rho_i.
$$

Learning $k$ coefficients is also cheaper than learning and repeatedly
projecting a full $d$-dimensional vector.

## Replacing the prompt token

Let the contextual CLIP conditioning sequence for target prompt $i$ be

$$
H_i = [h_{i,1}, h_{i,2}, \ldots, h_{i,L}],
$$

and let $m_i$ be the position of the final content token before the first
end-of-sequence token. Construct the modified sequence

$$
\widetilde H_i(c_i)_j =
\begin{cases}
a_i, & j=m_i, \\
h_{i,j}, & j\neq m_i.
\end{cases}
$$

This definition assumes that $a_i$ lives in the same contextual embedding
space as $h_{i,m_i}$. Replacing a contextual output of the text encoder is not
the same operation as replacing an input token embedding and running the text
encoder again. The implementation and experiments must state clearly which
space is being modified.

For multi-token concepts, the final content token may not represent the whole
concept. Two variants should therefore be tested:

- replace only the final content token;
- learn a shared displacement and apply it to every token in the concept span.

## Last-step differentiable generation

Let a deterministic sampler with $T$ denoising steps generate latent states

$$
x_T, x_{T-1}, \ldots, x_1, x_0.
$$

Use the modified conditioning $\widetilde H_i(c_i)$ throughout image
generation, but do not retain the computation graph for the first $T-1$
steps. Immediately before the final denoising step, detach the latent:

$$
\bar{x}_1 = \operatorname{stopgrad}(x_1).
$$

The final step remains differentiable:

$$
\widehat\epsilon_i
=
\epsilon_{\theta}
\left(
    \bar{x}_1,
    \tau_1,
    \widetilde H_i(c_i)
\right),
$$

$$
x_0
=
\operatorname{Step}
\left(
    \bar{x}_1,
    \widehat\epsilon_i,
    \tau_1
\right),
$$

and the decoded image is

$$
I_i = \operatorname{VAEdecode}(x_0).
$$

The diffusion model, VAE, text encoder, and CLIP model remain frozen. Only the
coefficient vector $c_i$ receives optimizer updates.

This is a one-step truncated-gradient estimator. The forward image depends on
the learned anchor at every denoising step, but the gradient measures only the
anchor's direct effect at the final step.

## CLIP objective

The objective uses two image branches generated from the same initial noise
and prompt template:

- a **learned-anchor branch**, conditioned on $\widetilde H_i(c_i)$;
- an **original-target branch**, conditioned on the unchanged target sequence
  $H_i$.

For seed $s$, define

$$
I_i^{a}(s)
=
G_{\theta}\left(x_T(s),\widetilde H_i(c_i)\right),
$$

$$
I_i^{t}(s)
=
\operatorname{stopgrad}
\left(
    G_{\theta}\left(x_T(s),H_i\right)
\right).
$$

Using the same $x_T(s)$ in both branches makes their difference primarily
attributable to the learned anchor rather than sampling noise. The original
target image does not depend on $c_i$, so it can be generated without
gradients and cached for a fixed set of training seeds.

Let $f_I$ be the frozen CLIP image encoder. The normalized image embeddings
are

$$
z_i^a
=
\frac{f_I(I_i^a)}{\lVert f_I(I_i^a) \rVert_2 + \epsilon},
$$

$$
z_i^t
=
\operatorname{stopgrad}
\left(
    \frac{f_I(I_i^t)}{\lVert f_I(I_i^t) \rVert_2 + \epsilon}
\right).
$$

The raw contextual token $a_i$ and a CLIP image embedding are not necessarily
in the same representation space or dimension. Therefore, their cosine
similarity must not be computed directly. Let $g_T$ denote the frozen CLIP
text pooling and projection operation that maps the modified prompt
representation into the joint CLIP image-text space. Define the normalized
learnable-anchor representation

$$
e_i^a
=
\frac{
    g_T\left(\widetilde H_i(c_i)\right)
}{
    \left\lVert
        g_T\left(\widetilde H_i(c_i)\right)
    \right\rVert_2+\epsilon
}.
$$

The proposed score maximizes agreement between the learnable anchor and its
own generated image while minimizing agreement between the learnable anchor
and the image generated by the original target prompt:

$$
\mathcal J_{\mathrm{contrast}}(c_i;s)
=
\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^a,e_i^a\right)
-\lambda_t
\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^t,e_i^a\right).
$$

Equivalently, minimize

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{contrast}}(c_i;s)
=
-\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^a,e_i^a\right)
+\lambda_t
\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^t,e_i^a\right).
$$

The first term makes the displaced anchor self-consistent with the images it
generates. The second term separates the displaced anchor from images
generated by the original target conditioning.

A margin-ranking form is preferable because it stops pushing the two scores
apart after sufficient separation has been achieved:

$$
\mathcal L_{\mathrm{rank}}(c_i;s)
=
\operatorname{ReLU}
\left(
    m
    -\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^a,e_i^a\right)
    +\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^t,e_i^a\right)
\right),
$$

where $m>0$ is the desired similarity margin. The constraint represented by
this loss is

$$
\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^a,e_i^a\right)
\geq
\operatorname{sim}\left(z_i^t,e_i^a\right)+m.
$$

This ranking objective directly expresses the intended behavior without
requiring either similarity to approach its extreme value.

### Coupled and detached anchor variants

In the fully coupled version, gradients reach $c_i$ through both $e_i^a$ and
$I_i^a$. This matches the proposed objective, but it permits a shortcut in
which the text representation moves to separate the two image embeddings more
quickly than the generated image changes.

An ablation should detach the anchor on the scoring branch:

$$
\bar e_i^a
=
\operatorname{stopgrad}\left(e_i^a\right).
$$

Substituting $\bar e_i^a$ for $e_i^a$ forces the current update to improve the
generated anchor image rather than directly rotating the scoring embedding.
The anchor representation is recomputed after every coefficient update, so it
can still evolve between iterations. Comparing coupled and detached variants
will reveal whether joint adaptation provides useful feedback or an easy
optimization shortcut.

## Interpretation of the objective

CLIP similarity is not a probability density and should not be presented as a
direct estimate of

$$
p_{\theta}(I \mid a_i).
$$

The proposed objective instead finds a direction that is **self-consistent
with its generated images**, **separated from the original target images**,
and **locally realizable by the diffusion model** under the selected sampler,
seeds, and prompts. A careful claim is:

> We optimize a direction in the semantic displacement space so that the
> displaced anchor aligns more strongly with its own generated images than
> with images generated by the original target prompt.

If a true density or likelihood interpretation is required, the objective
would need to use a diffusion likelihood bound, a noise-prediction loss over
timesteps, or another calibrated density surrogate.

## Main concern with using only the final step

The final denoising step usually makes a relatively small correction to an
image whose global semantic content was established earlier. Consequently,
the last-step gradient may be:

- too weak to reveal which anchor changes the generated concept;
- biased toward texture and fine detail rather than global semantics;
- inconsistent with the anchor's cumulative effect over the complete
  denoising trajectory.

This does not make the idea invalid. It makes the last-step objective a cheap
surrogate whose quality must be measured.

Two stronger variants preserve most of the memory advantage:

1. **Last-$K$-step gradient:** detach at $x_K$ and differentiate through the
   final $K$ denoising steps.
2. **Random-step gradient:** sample a timestep at each iteration, detach its
   incoming latent, and differentiate through that denoising step. Across
   iterations, the learned direction receives information from the entire
   trajectory.

The last-step version should remain the first prototype because it is the
simplest and cheapest test of whether image-level supervision provides a
useful signal.

## Proposed optimization algorithm

For each target concept $i$:

1. Build the orthonormal semantic displacement basis $B$.
2. Compute $\rho_i$ as the mean norm of the residuals from target $t_i$ to all
   explicitly defined anchors; do not include residuals to other targets.
3. Initialize $c_i$ from an anchor-directed direction, a projected
   negative-target direction, or a fixed random seed.
4. Normalize $c_i$ and construct $a_i=t_i+\rho_iB^{\top}c_i/\lVert c_i\rVert$.
5. Replace the chosen content token in the contextual conditioning sequence.
6. Sample one or more initial noise latents and use the same noise for both
   image branches.
7. Generate and cache the original-target images without gradients.
8. Generate the learned-anchor branch through the truncation boundary without
   retaining gradients.
9. Detach the boundary latent and enable gradients for the final denoising
   step or final $K$ steps.
10. Decode the learned-anchor image with the frozen VAE.
11. Project the learned anchor into the joint CLIP text-image space and compute
    its similarity to both image branches.
12. Compute the contrastive margin-ranking objective.
13. Backpropagate only to $c_i$ and take an optimizer step.
14. Repeat for the desired number of optimizer steps.
15. Save the final residual

$$
r_i=\rho_iB^{\top}
\frac{c_i}{\lVert c_i\rVert_2+\epsilon}
$$

for use in the SPEED closed-form model edit.

## First experiment

Start with a small few-concept benchmark and compare:

1. negative-target projection;
2. anchor-directed projection;
3. CLIP-guided last-step anchor search;
4. CLIP-guided last-$K$-step search for a small $K$;
5. CLIP-guided random-step search.

Keep the semantic displacement basis, residual rank, mean-norm computation,
SPEED threshold, seeds, and evaluation prompts fixed across methods.

Suggested initial settings:

- optimize basis coefficients rather than a full embedding vector;
- freeze every model parameter;
- use a deterministic DDIM-style sampler during optimization;
- start from the anchor-directed projected direction;
- use paired learned-anchor and original-target images generated from the same
  initial noise;
- start with the margin-ranking objective and compare coupled versus detached
  anchor scoring;
- begin with the final step, then test $K \in \{2,4,8\}$ if its gradient is
  weak.

Report:

- learned-image-to-anchor and target-image-to-anchor similarities;
- the achieved training and held-out similarity margins;
- target erasure and non-target retention after the SPEED edit;
- cosine similarity between the learned and initial directions;
- gradient norm with respect to $c_i$;
- image change caused by the final differentiable step;
- sensitivity to seed, prompt template, token position, and initialization;
- optimization time and peak GPU memory.

## Success criteria

The idea is promising if the learned residual:

- generalizes to seeds and prompts not used during optimization;
- improves erasure after the SPEED update without harming retention;
- remains effective when evaluated with metrics other than the training CLIP
  model;
- produces stable directions across different initializations;
- gives a measurable gradient through the selected denoising step;
- outperforms simple anchor projection enough to justify image-generation
  optimization.

## Summary

The proposed method turns residual selection into an image-supervised search:

$$
\text{semantic displacement space}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{learnable unit direction}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{displaced anchor}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{generated image}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\text{contrastive CLIP supervision}.
$$

Its main strength is that it uses the diffusion model's actual image response
to choose a direction within the semantic displacement space. Its main risk is
that a gradient from only the final denoising step may describe local image
refinement rather than the anchor's full semantic effect. The initial
experiment should determine whether this inexpensive surrogate is sufficient
before expanding to multiple or randomly sampled denoising steps.
